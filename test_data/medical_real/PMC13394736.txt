ABSTRACT 

Esophagogastric junction (EGJ) cancer is an increasing health burden worldwide. Over the past decade, significant advancements in centralization, multimodal treatment strategies, as well as the widespread adoption of minimally invasive surgical techniques, have led to marked improvements in perioperative outcomes and overall survival of EGJ cancer in the Netherlands. Perioperative treatment strategies have evolved toward intensified systemic therapy, with perioperative chemotherapy, particularly the FLOT regimen. The preferred approach for surgical treatment of EGJ cancer remains debated, however, minimally invasive transthoracic techniques are increasingly adopted and have led to superior radicality and decreased morbidity. Centralization of care with specialized multidisciplinary teams has been consistently associated with improved surgical quality and survival in the Netherlands, as confirmed by the registry‐based nationwide quality control.

This review summarizes current evidence regarding diagnosis, staging, perioperative therapy, surgical management, and centralization of care from a Dutch perspective.

1 

Introduction 

Esophagogastric junction (EGJ) carcinoma represents a major global health burden, ranked amongst the leading causes of cancer‐related mortality worldwide [ 1 ]. The incidence of EGJ cancer in the Netherlands is amidst the highest in Europe, and is increasing in recent years [ 2 , 3 ]. 

Over the past decade, significant advancements in centralization, multimodal treatment strategies, as well as the widespread adoption of minimally invasive surgical techniques, have led to marked improvements in perioperative outcomes and overall survival in the Netherlands. This review provides a structured, multidisciplinary overview of EGJ cancer management from a Dutch perspective, highlighting how national practices and research initiatives align with or diverge from international practices. Beyond summarizing existing evidence, it identifies practical insights and lessons from Dutch experience that may inform and optimize patient care in other settings. 

 2 

Epidemiology and Prognosis 

The annual incidence of EGJ cancer in the Netherlands is approximately 750 patients, corresponding to 4.1 new cases per 100 000 inhabitants (Figure 1 ), which is amongst the highest incidence rates in Europe [ 2 , 3 ]. The Dutch EGJ incidence, similar to other countries, is increasing over recent years [ 3 , 4 ]. The major risk factors in Western populations include excess body weight, Barrett's Esophagus (BE), and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [ 5 , 6 ]. The Dutch patient population predominantly consists of men, older than 65 years, with adenocarcinoma histological subtype [ 7 ]. FIGURE 1 

EGJ cancer incidence and median overall survival in the Netherlands (2010–2025). 

The prognosis of EGJ, although relatively poor, has steadily improved over the past decades, with a median overall survival in the Netherlands of approximately 35 months after diagnosis, including curative and advanced disease stages (Figure 1 ) [ 7 ]. These survival improvements are largely attributable to centralization of esophagogastric cancer care, increased surgical quality, and advancements in multimodal treatment selection [ 7 , 8 , 9 ]. 

 3 

Diagnosis, Pathology, and Staging 

The primary diagnostic modality for EGJ cancer consists of upper gastrointestinal endoscopy with biopsy sampling, according to the European guidelines, requiring minimally six to eight representative biopsies of suspected lesions for histopathological and molecular assessment [ 8 ]. The predominant histological subtypes of upper gastrointestinal malignancies are squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and adenocarcinoma, with SCC generally classified as esophageal cancer [ 8 , 10 ]. Diagnosis should be histopathological confirmed, according to the World Health Organization histological tumor type classification, which closely resembles to the classification scheme of the Japanese Gastric Cancer Association [ 8 , 11 , 12 , 13 ]. Comprehensive molecular pathological assessment, based on The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) classification, includes distinction between Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)‐positive tumors, microsatellite instability–high (MSI‐H) tumors, genomically stable (GS) tumors, and tumors characterized by chromosomal instability (CIN), including HER2 amplification [ 13 , 14 ]. The clinical relevance of standardized testing of PD‐L1 in the curative setting is still under evaluation, however is widely performed in the Netherlands [ 13 ]. The TCGA classification shows some overlap with the Asian Cancer Research Group subtype classification [ 13 , 14 , 15 ]. 

In the Netherlands, staging of EGJ cancer is performed according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) and International Union for Cancer Control (UICC) staging manual (Figure 2 for a schematic overview) [ 16 ]. Contrast‐enhanced CT or FDG‐PET‐CT is performed to assess primary tumor (T), nodal (N) and metastatic (M) disease stage [ 8 ]. Endoscopic ultrasound or endobronchial ultrasonography may be performed on indication [ 8 ]. Locally advanced EGJ cancer, staged T3‐4 with significant gastric invasion, may opt for staging laparoscopy to rule out peritoneal metastases [ 17 ]. FIGURE 2 

Schematic overview of diagnostic workup of EGJ Cancer in the Netherlands. 

 3.1 

Primary Tumor 

The differentiation between esophageal‐ and EGJ‐originating adenocarcinoma is of predominant value since it may influence the choice of neoadjuvant treatment, surgical approach and extent of lymphadenectomy [ 10 , 18 ]. However, accurate determination can be troubled due to the anatomical location in the transitional zone between the esophagus and stomach [ 19 ]. The Siewert classification has historically been widely applied to categorize EGJ adenocarcinomas based on the location of the tumor epicenter relative to the esophagogastric junction, distinguishing type I adenocarcinoma of the distal esophagus (epicenter one to five proximal of the EGJ), type II adenocarcinoma of the cardia (epicenter 1 cm above to 2 cm below the EGJ), and type III sub‐cardiac adenocarcinoma (epicenter 2–5 cm below the EGJ) [ 20 ]. Currently, the eighth edition of the AJCC/UICC staging manual is the preferred classification system for EGJ adenocarcinoma. According to this classification, tumors with an epicenter located ≤ 2 cm distal to the EGJ are staged as esophageal adenocarcinomas, whereas tumors with an epicenter located > 2 cm distal to the EGJ are staged as gastric adenocarcinomas (Figure 3 ) [ 10 ]. Emerging data suggest that molecular and genetic tumor signatures may allow for more accurate determination of tumor origin, and such approaches are anticipated to be incorporated into future editions of the AJCC/UICC staging manual [ 10 , 14 ]. This determination could be crucial since the origin of the primary tumor could affect the selection of neoadjuvant or surgical treatment regimen. 

 3.2 

Lymph Node Staging 

The presence of lymph node metastases is among the most important prognostic factors in EGJ cancer [ 21 ]. Accurate nodal staging is crucial in determining prognosis and detrimental for selecting multimodal treatment and surgical strategies [ 22 ]. Different nodal classification systems exist worldwide, however the eighth edition of the AJCC/UICC staging system and the twelfth edition of the Japanese Classification of Esophageal Cancer (JES) are commonly applied. According to the eighth edition of the AJCC/UICC staging system, periesophageal lymph nodes—extending from the cervical to the celiac region—are generally regarded as regional nodes. In Europe, extra‐regional lymph nodes are classified as metastatic disease (M1), and are therefore not eligible for curative‐intent treatment [ 10 , 23 ]. This classification is independent of the location of the primary tumor, in contrast to the twelfth edition of the JES, in which extra‐regional lymph nodes are subdivided into M1a and M1b, depending on the potential therapeutic benefit if resected [ 10 , 23 ]. These nodal staging classifications, besides this conceptual difference, are largely comparable, since both classifications use the number of suspected lymph nodes to determine N stage, ranging from N0 to N3. 

Prospective multicenter studies evaluating lymph node metastasis patterns in EGJ cancer demonstrated that the distribution of nodal metastases strongly correlates with the anatomical location and extent of esophageal involvement of the primary tumor [ 24 ]. Patients with greater esophageal invasion more frequently harbor mediastinal lymph node metastases, whereas tumors with predominant gastric involvement more commonly metastasize to abdominal nodal stations [ 24 ]. These findings support tailoring the extent of lymphadenectomy according to tumor location and have further highlighted the limitations of a uniform surgical approach for all esophageal and EGJ cancers. The ongoing international TIGER study (Figure 4 ), investigating the distribution patterns of lymph node metastases in over 6000 patients, is highly anticipated, as it is expected to define global lymph node metastasis patterns and establish evidence‐based recommendations regarding nodal staging, and the optimal extent of lymphadenectomy [ 25 ]. The MAGIC trial was the amongst the first randomized studies evaluating different perioperative chemotherapy regimens for EGJ cancer and demonstrated a potential survival benefit [ 26 ]. However, interpretation of the results was limited by relatively small sample size and lack of standardized surgical quality assessment [ 26 ]. 

The Dutch CROSS trial introduced the neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) CROSS regimen for locally advanced esophageal and EGJ cancer (Siewert I‐II), demonstrating superior short‐ and long‐term survival compared to surgery alone [ 27 , 28 ]. Following publication of the CROSS trial, the CROSS regimen was widely implemented for esophageal and EGJ cancer in Western countries. Nevertheless, subgroup analyses failed to demonstrate five‐year survival benefit in adenocarcinoma patients, female patients, and cN+ disease, and outcomes for the specific EGJ subgroup were not separately reported [ 27 ]. Furthermore, concerns have been raised regarding the surgical quality achieved in the CROSS trial. The median lymph node yield remained below the established textbook outcome threshold of ≥ 20 resected lymph nodes in both treatment arms, with a median of 15 and 18 lymph nodes retrieved in the nCRT and surgery‐alone groups, respectively [ 34 ]. In addition, the R0 resection rate in the nCRT arm was only 69%, well below the average of the Dutch Upper GI Cancer Audit 93% [ 35 ]. When adjusting for surgical quality, the real‐world survival benefit of additional radiotherapy diminishes [ 36 ]. This emphasizes the critical importance of high‐quality surgery in esophageal and EGJ cancer management. 

Additional concerns emerged following publication of the ten‐year CROSS trial follow‐up results, which demonstrated comparable distant metastases rates between the nCRT and surgery‐alone groups, suggesting that chemoradiotherapy may provide insufficient systemic disease control [ 28 ]. This observation renewed interest in intensified systemic chemotherapy strategies. 

Similar findings were reported in the Japanese JCOG1109 NExT trial, in which neoadjuvant triplet chemotherapy demonstrated superior survival compared with doublet chemotherapy plus radiotherapy [ 37 ]. However, this trial exclusively included Asian patients with squamous cell carcinoma, thereby limiting its generalizability to Western populations with esophageal and EGJ adenocarcinoma [ 37 ]. 

The FLOT4 trial subsequently investigated the preferred perioperative chemotherapy regimen for locally advanced EGJ cancer in a Western population, and demonstrated the superiority of fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin and docetaxel (FLOT) in overall survival compared to either fluorouracil or capecitabine, plus cisplatin and epirubicin (ECF or ECX) [ 29 ]. 

 4.2 

Neoadjuvant Chemoradiotherapy Versus Perioperative Chemotherapy 

Several comparative studies evaluating neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy versus perioperative chemotherapy demonstrated higher rates of anastomotic leakage, major postoperative morbidity, and mortality following chemoradiotherapy, while chemotherapy was associated with increased systemic toxicity [ 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 ]. Adequate comparison was limited by the inconsistent reporting of survival outcomes across these studies. 

The CRITICS trial, investigating postoperative chemotherapy versus chemoradiotherapy in gastric cancer patients following neoadjuvant chemotherapy and surgery, demonstrated no survival benefit of additional radiotherapy [ 30 ]. Similarly, the Neo‐AEGIS phase III trial, comparing neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy with perioperative chemotherapy in esophageal and EGJ cancer, failed to demonstrate significant differences in overall survival, although the study was terminated prematurely [ 31 ]. These findings are supported by EGJ subgroup analyses of the TOPGEAR trial, comparing perioperative chemoradiotherapy with perioperative chemotherapy in locally advanced gastric cancer [ 32 ]. 

More recently, the German ESOPEC trial further supported the hypothesis that enhanced systemic chemotherapy may be superior to the addition of radiotherapy, demonstrating improved survival with perioperative FLOT compared with neoadjuvant CROSS in patients with esophageal and EGJ cancer [ 44 ]. However, this superiority was statistically significant only for esophageal and Siewert type I tumors, and not for Siewert type II–III EGJ cancers [ 44 ]. 

 4.3 

Dutch Practice 

In the Netherlands, patients with EGJ classified esophageal adenocarcinoma, according to the eight AJCC (Siewert type I–II tumors), are generally treated by either the CROSS or FLOT regimen (Figure 3 ). Although institutional practices may vary, perioperative FLOT is typically preferred in patients with extensive nodal disease (cTxN2–3M0), whereas nCRT according to CROSS is commonly reserved for patients with limited nodal involvement (cTxN0–1M0), or when completion of perioperative chemotherapy is considered unfeasible [ 45 ]. The preferred treatment strategy for EGJ classified gastric adenocarcinoma (Siewert type III) tumors is generally perioperative FLOT [ 45 ]. 

 5 

Active Surveillance Following Chemoradiotherapy 

The CROSS trial reported a pathological complete response (pCR) rate of approximately 30% in patients treated with neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) followed by surgery, raising the question whether esophagectomy, an intervention associated with substantial morbidity, may be unnecessary in a subset of patients [ 27 ]. The recently published Dutch SANO trial evaluated the feasibility of an active surveillance strategy in patients with esophageal and EGJ cancer who achieved a clinically complete response following nCRT, with the aim of omitting the incidence of futile surgery in patients with a high likelihood of pCR [ 46 ]. Two‐year follow‐up of the SANO trial showed non‐inferior overall survival of active surveillance versus standard surgery following nCRT in clinically complete responders [ 46 ]. 

However, several important limitations restrict implementation of active surveillance strategies in EGJ cancer. First, accurate detection of residual disease after nCRT remains challenging. In the preSANO study, approximately 10% of patients with substantial residual tumor burden (tumor regression grade 3 and 4) were not identified during clinical response evaluations, while false‐negative biopsies occurred in up to 55% of cases [ 47 ]. Second, only a limited proportion of subjects remained eligible for active surveillance throughout the SANO trial. Of the 776 included patients, merely 69 patient (9%) ultimately underwent active surveillance, thereby limiting the general applicability of this strategy [ 46 ]. Third, follow‐up data remains relatively immature. The SANO trial reported a median follow‐up of approximately two years, which is insufficient to adequately assess long‐term recurrence patterns and overall survival [ 46 ]. Moreover, generally wide non‐inferiority margin of 15% complicates interpretation of long‐term oncologic results, since the statistical analyses were planned based on five‐year survival estimates, whereas the duration of follow‐up was insufficient to evaluate this outcome [ 46 ]. Finally, EGJ cancer patients represented only approximately 25% of the SANO population, while subgroup‐specific outcomes for EGJ tumors have not yet been separately reported [ 46 ]. 

Therefore, based on the currently available evidence, active surveillance following nCRT should not (yet) be considered standard treatment for patients with EGJ cancer outside experienced centers or clinical trials. 

 6 

Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors and Molecular‐Targeted Therapies 

 6.1 

Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors 

The Checkmate‐577 trial evaluated adjuvant nivolumab in patients with resected esophageal or esophagogastric junction cancer who had residual disease following neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy [ 48 , 49 ]. Initial analyses demonstrated improved disease‐free survival, leading to temporary implementation of adjuvant nivolumab in Dutch clinical practice [ 48 ]. However, subsequent analyses after prolonged follow‐up failed to demonstrate a significant overall survival benefit [ 49 ]. Furthermore, outcomes in the control arm appeared inferior compared with contemporaneous Dutch national registry data, leading to discontinuation of routine adjuvant nivolumab treatment in the Netherlands [ 48 , 49 ]. 

The BASALT trial was a proof‐of‐concept study, investigating the feasibility of additional tocilizumab to nCRT in esophageal adenocarcinoma patients to increase the efficacy of chemoradiation [ 50 ]. However, the trial was discontinued due to increased risk of esophageal perforation, potentially due to the inhibiting effect on IL‐6, thereby impairing wound healing [ 50 ]. 

More recently, the MATTERHORN trial investigated the addition of durvalumab to perioperative FLOT chemotherapy in resectable gastric and EGJ adenocarcinoma [ 51 ]. The results demonstrated significantly increased event‐free survival and complete response rates following durvalumab plus FLOT, supporting the growing role of immunotherapy combined with systemic chemotherapy in localized EGJ cancer [ 51 ]. These findings may further expand perioperative treatment strategies in biomarker‐selected patients. 

 6.2 

 HER2 ‐Targeted Therapy 

The phase II TRAP study demonstrated the feasibility of combining neoadjuvant trastuzumab and pertuzumab with chemoradiotherapy in patients with HER‐2 positive esophageal adenocarcinoma, and although the study was not formally powered to assess survival endpoints, a benefit was suggested [ 52 ]. The ongoing phase III TRAP‐2 trial is currently evaluating whether dual HER2 blockade combined with chemoradiotherapy improves long‐term oncologic outcomes in HER2‐positive esophageal and EGJ adenocarcinoma [ 53 ]. 

 7 

Surgical Treatment 

Despite the rising incidence of EGJ cancer, standardized surgical treatment is yet established due to the anatomical location in the transitional zone between esophagus and stomach. 

 7.1 

Surgical Approach, Radicality, and Lymphadenectomy 

Several techniques are available for EGJ cancer, including transthoracic esophagectomy, transhiatal esophagectomy, total gastrectomy or transhiatal extended gastrectomy. Selection of the surgical approach is based on tumor location, extent of esophageal invasion, and institutional expertise, as perioperative morbidity and mortality are generally comparable between these techniques [ 54 ]. 

Concerns, however, have been raised regarding whether adequate oncologic radicality can be achieved with total gastrectomy or transhiatal extended gastrectomy, given that mediastinal lymph node metastases occur in up to 28% of patients with EGJ tumors [ 55 ]. As discussed previously, lymph node metastases are amongst the most important prognostic determinants in EGJ cancer, and extensive lymphadenectomy has been associated with improved long‐term survival, suggesting enhanced oncologic radicality if extensive lymphadenectomy is performed [ 56 , 57 ]. 

These findings suggest that EGJ cancers with esophageal involvement (as risk factor for mediastinal lymph node metastases) may benefit from a transthoracic Ivor Lewis esophagectomy or a left thoracoabdominal approach. These approaches facilitate increased exposure, higher lymph node yields, and improved R0 resection rates, compared to transhiatal approaches [ 57 , 58 ]. This is supported by the 2023 Upper GI Oncology Summit recommendations, which (weakly) recommend lower mediastinal lymph node dissection in patients with EGJ cancer and esophageal invasion [ 59 ]. 

The Dutch nationwide IVORY study demonstrated a marked shift towards transthoracic esophagectomy over recent decades, from 41% to 81% [ 7 , 60 ]. This nationwide trend was accompanied by significant improvements in perioperative outcomes and overall survival (Table 2 ), reflecting the increasing emphasis on oncologic radicality [ 7 , 60 ]. Consequently, the preferred surgical strategy in the Netherlands has evolved according to tumor location. The preferred surgical technique for EGJ esophageal adenocarcinoma (Siewert type I–II) tumors is a transthoracic esophagectomy, whereas EGJ gastric adenocarcinoma (Siewert type III) tumors are more commonly treated with total gastrectomy, or transhiatal extended gastrectomy (Figure 3 ). In experienced high‐volume Dutch centers, the left thoracoabdominal approach is a well‐established surgical technique for selected EGJ adenocarcinoma patients, providing excellent exposure of the distal esophagus, esophagogastric junction, and proximal stomach [ 62 ]. 

The ongoing CARDIA trial is expected to provide high‐level evidence regarding the optimal surgical strategy for Siewert type II tumors, as it is the first Western randomized trial that directly compares transthoracic esophagectomy with transhiatal extended gastrectomy [ 63 ]. The results of this study are anticipated to further refine surgical decision‐making for EGJ cancer subgroups. 

 7.2 

Minimally Invasive Approach 

The surgical techniques applied in Upper GI surgery have evolved significantly in the last decades with the transition from open to minimally invasive surgery (7%–92%), and currently expends to robot‐assisted minimally invasive surgery [ 7 , 60 ]. Multiple randomized trials, including TIME, and MIRO, consistently demonstrated superior perioperative outcomes for minimally invasive and robot‐assisted approaches compared with open surgery. The TIME trial demonstrated significant decreased pulmonary complications and shorter length of stay following the minimally invasive approach, although overall survival and lymph node yield were comparable [ 55 ]. Data from the Dutch Upper GI Cancer Audit, amongst others, confirmed the reduced length of stay, decreased morbidity, and comparable survival, however, superior lymph node yield was found following MIE [ 64 ]. The MIRO trial showed a significant benefit of minimally invasive approach on postoperative health related quality of life (HRQoL) [ 65 , 66 ]. Ultimately, supporting the transition from open to minimally invasive approaches. 

Regarding the minimally invasive versus robot‐assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy, no randomized controlled trials thus far have been completed that support robot‐assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy over minimally invasive esophagectomy for esophageal cancer. Several observational cohort studies suggested a potential reduction in postoperative complications favoring RAMIE [ 67 , 68 ]. The ROBOT‐2 trial is ongoing and will potentially contribute to this discussion [ 69 ]. 

New even less invasive techniques like single‐port robot‐assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy (SP RAMIE) or minimally invasive transcervical esophagectomy (MICE) are currently still under evaluation for esophageal and EGJ cancers, however are limited by a long learning curve [ 70 , 71 ]. 

 7.3 

Preferred Surgical Treatment 

In the Amsterdam UMC, the preferred surgical treatment for EGJ cancers generally consists of Ivor‐Lewis esophagectomy for Siewert type I, and either Ivor Lewis or left thoracoabdominal esophagectomy for Siewert type II, both with two‐field lymphadenectomy given the frequent involvement of mediastinal lymph node stations (Figure 3 ) [ 55 ]. 

 8 

Health‐Related Quality of Life 

Multimodal treatment for esophageal and EGJ cancer can be a significant burden for patients, associated with reduced health‐related quality of life (HR‐QoL), and could potentially be used for clinical decision making in case of multiple valid treatment options [ 72 , 73 ]. This patient preferences in decision counseling, if clinically possible and responsible, could increase patient‐reported satisfaction [ 74 ]. 

Recent studies have increasingly used HR‐QoL outcome measures to compare treatment strategies. The LASER study, investigating lasting symptoms after esophageal resection, demonstrated a comparable clinical decrease in HR‐QoL between perioperative chemotherapy and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy [ 75 ]. Long‐term health‐related quality of life is expected to differ between surgical approaches. However, comparative studies evaluating total gastrectomy versus Ivor Lewis esophagectomy have demonstrated substantial impact on QoL following both procedures. Patients undergoing gastrectomy more frequently reported odynophagia, early satiety, reflux symptoms, and difficulties with oral intake, whereas esophagectomy patients experienced higher rates of insomnia, swallowing‐related choking, coughing, and respiratory complaints [ 76 ]. Interestingly, when looking into the data of the ongoing Dutch Prospective Observational Cohort Study of Esophageal‐Gastric Cancer Patients (POCOP), no significant difference in HR‐QoL was found between patients with and without complications after multimodal treatment for esophageal of EGJ cancer [ 77 ]. 

 9 

Centralization 

Multidisciplinary treatment and surgical quality are a critical determinants of outcomes in upper gastrointestinal malignancies, with a particularly strong impact on survival [ 36 ]. Centralization of esophagogastric cancer care, and annual volume requirements have been consistently associated with improved survival outcomes [ 78 ]. This relationship is generally attributed to specialized multidisciplinary teams delivering higher‐quality, guideline‐adherent care supported by greater surgical expertise, optimized perioperative management, and structured postoperative rehabilitation pathways [ 78 ]. 

In the Netherlands, centralization of esophageal cancer surgery was initiated in 2006 with implementation of a minimum annual hospital volume threshold of at least 10 esophagectomies per center. In 2011, this threshold was increased to 20 annual resections and coincided with establishment of the Dutch Upper Gastrointestinal Cancer Audit (DUCA), a mandatory nationwide surgical registry including all esophagogastric cancer patients undergoing surgery with curative intent. These developments triggered a nationwide wave of centralization, which was largely completed by 2019, when the number of Dutch esophagectomy centers had decreased to 16 and all centers fulfilled the annual minimum volume requirement (Figure 5 ) [ 81 ]. Registry‐based analyses from the Dutch Upper GI Cancer Audit demonstrated significant associations between increasing hospital volume and improved surgical outcomes [ 81 ]. When evaluating hypothetical higher annual volume thresholds, centers performing ≥ 53 esophagectomies annually demonstrated lower rates of technical complications (aOR 0.82; 95% CI 0.70–0.96), anastomotic leakage (aOR 0.80; 95% CI 0.66–0.97), and intensive care unit stay (aOR 0.80; 95% CI 0.69–0.93), alongside improved textbook outcome (aOR 1.25; 95% CI 1.07–1.46) and higher lymph node yield (aOR 3.56; 95% CI 2.68–4.77) [ 81 ]. Most volume‐outcomes plateaued at 50–60 annual resections, but lymph node yield and anastomotic leakage continued to improve by further volume increase [ 81 ]. 

Consequently, effective January 1st, 2026, the Netherlands implemented an increased annual minimum threshold of 75 combined esophageal and gastric cancer resections per center, aiming to further enhance patient outcomes through concentration of expertise. 

In contrast, volume requirements across Europe remain highly heterogeneous, with approximately 25% of countries lacking formal centralization criteria, underscoring a significant opportunity for improvement [ 79 ]. European countries with centralized healthcare systems tend to demonstrate superior oncologic outcomes, attributable to standardized care pathways, improved adherence to evidence‐based guidelines, and the availability of comprehensive national cancer registries [ 79 ].

Future Directions and Ongoing Trials 

Several ongoing studies aim to further optimize staging and perioperative treatment strategies specifically for EGJ cancer. The TIGER study is expected to give a crucial insight into the worldwide nodal dissemination pattern of esophageal and EGJ cancer, giving crucial insights for improved staging and lymphadenectomy [ 25 , 82 ]. 

The NEO‐JPEG trial is currently evaluating the preferred neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimen for EGJ by comparing FLOT with docetaxel, oxaliplatin and S‐1 (DOS) [ 83 ]. The German RACE and Dutch TNT‐OES‐II trials investigate different combinations of the neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and FLOT regimen, to potentially maximize both the local and systemic treatment effect [ 84 , 85 ]. The ongoing phase III DANTE and KEYNOTE‐585 trials evaluate the addition of, respectively, atezolizumab and pembrolizumab, to perioperative chemotherapy regimens in resectable gastric and EGJ adenocarcinoma, reflecting the growing role of immunotherapy in the curative setting. 

The CARDIA trial evaluates the optimal surgical approach for Siewert type II EGJ cancer by randomizing between transthoracic esophagectomy and transhiatal extended gastrectomy. The ROBOT‐2 trial is expected to support decision making between minimally invasive or robot‐assisted minimally invasive esophagectomy [ 69 ]. The upcoming TUPEC and IGLO trials are expected to further optimize surgical techniques by evaluating the role of thoracic duct resection versus preservation in EGJ surgery, and fluorescence guided surgery for patient‐tailored lymphadenectomy. 

Future treatment strategies are expected to increasingly incorporate molecular biomarkers such as HER2 amplification, microsatellite instability‐high (MSI‐H) status, PD‐L1 expression, and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) to improve patient selection and treatment personalization. In addition, advances in translational profiling and response prediction may further facilitate individualized treatment strategies, allowing improved selection of patients for chemotherapy, chemoradiotherapy, immunotherapy, or organ‐preserving approaches in EGJ cancer. 

 11 

Summary 

Esophagogastric junction cancer represents a growing global health burden, with particularly high incidence rates in the Netherlands. Advances in diagnostic modalities, molecular characterization, and staging have improved disease stratification, and multidisciplinary treatment selection. Multimodal treatment strategies, including perioperative chemotherapy and neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy have significantly improved survival. Emerging evidence increasingly supports intensified systemic chemotherapy strategies, particularly in patients with extensive nodal disease, and personalized treatment approaches. 

Surgical management remains complex due to the anatomical location between the esophagus and stomach. Growing evidence supports transthoracic and minimally invasive esophagectomy in appropriately selected patients, particularly in tumors with esophageal involvement and mediastinal lymph node dissemination. 

The Netherlands has played a leading role in centralization of esophagogastric cancer care. Nationwide implementation of minimum hospital volume thresholds, multidisciplinary treatment planning, and mandatory registry‐based quality monitoring through the Dutch Upper Gastrointestinal Cancer Audit have been associated with significant improvements in perioperative and oncologic outcomes. 

Although emerging strategies such as immunotherapy, targeted therapy, biomarker‐driven treatment selection, and active surveillance show promise, their role in EGJ cancer remains under active investigation. Continued refinement of centralized, multidisciplinary, and individualized care pathways is essential to further improve outcomes for patients with EGJ cancer.
