Acute heart failure (AHF) is a systemic condition characterised by multiorgan involvement that varies from subclinical to overt organ failure. The liver is particularly vulnerable due to its dual blood supply and sensitivity to haemodynamic and inflammatory disturbances. Abnormal liver function tests are highly prevalent in patients with AHF, reflecting major mechanisms: passive hepatic venous congestion from elevated central venous pressure, hepatocellular ischaemia from low cardiac output, systemic inflammation and poor metabolic status associated with cachexia. In patients with AHF, liver function tests at admission, during hospitalisation and at discharge are independently associated with rehospitalisation, cardiovascular and all-cause mortality. Persistent abnormalities after hospitalisation often reflect incomplete decongestion or irreversible structural liver changes. Therapeutic strategies focus on decongestion, optimisation of cardiac output and medication adjustments to avoid hepatotoxicity. Despite significant advances in understanding cardio-hepatic interactions, major gaps remain, including the lack of standardised definitions, limited mechanistic studies and the underrepresentation of patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction in contemporary research. Future research should explore targeted therapies to improve cardiohepatic interactions and patient outcomes.

Acute heart failure (AHF) is a systemic disease with multiorgan involvement (e.g. vasculature, kidney, liver). 1 Among these, the liver is a highly vulnerable organ due to its unique dual blood supply and sensitivity to haemodynamic alterations in venous pressure, perfusion and systemic inflammatory status. 

Abnormalities in liver function tests (LFTs) are frequently detected in patients with AHF, yet their clinical significance remains underappreciated. Over time, increasing evidence has highlighted their diagnostic, prognostic and pathophysiological relevance. These abnormalities reflect the interplay among congestion, hypoperfusion and systemic inflammation, and are associated with worse outcomes across heart failure (HF) with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). 2 – 4 

This paper integrates data from registries, randomised clinical trials (RCTs), consensus documents and mechanistic studies to provide a contemporary review of the epidemiology, pathophysiology, biochemical phenotypes and clinical implications of LFT abnormalities in AHF, linking the site of origin of hepatic biomarkers to their haemodynamic consequences, offering a new perspective on hepatic injury. 

Dynamic monitoring of LFT trajectories may serve as a practical tool to differentiate reversible from irreversible dysfunction, thereby refining both prognosis and treatment strategies in patients with AHF. 

Epidemiology 

LFTs encompass a broad range of biochemical markers reflecting hepatocellular injury, cholestasis and synthetic capacity ( Figure 1 ). The most commonly assessed LFTs are alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT), total bilirubin (BIL), and direct BIL, albumin (ALB) and prothrombin time or international normalised ratio (INR). 

Data from AHF registries and RCTs suggest that up to 46% of hospitalised patients have abnormal LFTs at presentation, although the prevalence varies across studies ( Figure 2 ), because different thresholds have been used to define ‘abnormal’ LFTs. 2 , 3 , 5 Generally, peak transaminase and BIL values are higher in registries than in RCTs because ALT or AST levels more than three times the upper limit of normal (ULN) and BIL levels more than two times the ULN are exclusion criteria in the majority of AHF clinical trials. Particularly in RCTs, inclusion criteria select populations with varying baseline severity indices, explaining the variation in the prevalence of abnormal LFTs at presentation. The observed difference in the prevalence of cytolytic vs cholestatic enzymes depends on clinical profiles (more congestion vs more hypoperfusion) of patients enrolled in clinical trials ( Figure 2 ). In addition, the timing of collection of LFT data (i.e. at admission or at randomisation) varies across trials. In addition, the type of abnormality differs: cholestatic markers (BIL, ALP, GGT) are more frequently elevated than transaminases, because AST and ALT elevations typically reflect acute hypoperfusion-related injury. In a post hoc analysis of the SURVIVE trial, enrolling high-risk AHF patients treated with dobutamine or levosimendan, abnormal LFTs were observed in 46% of patients, with AST and ALT elevations being the most prevalent (26%). 3 These findings are consistent with those of other studies. 2 , 4 , 6 – 8 Finally, a full panel of LFTs was not collected in observational or randomised studies, limiting interstudy comparison. 

Increased BIL is common in patients with AHF and is strongly associated with worsening HF, cardiovascular death and all-cause mortality. 4 , 7 , 9 , 10 

Abnormal GGT levels are highly prevalent among hospitalised AHF patients, particularly those with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction. In the EVEREST trial, which enrolled 2,061 patients admitted for worsening HF with left ventricular ejection fraction ≤40%, 62% of patients had abnormal baseline GGT levels (>50 IU/l). 7 This prevalence remained unchanged at discharge or day 7 (still 62%), despite improvements in other markers of congestion. 7 

Hypoalbuminaemia is another common LFT abnormality (prevalence between 17% and 54%), strongly associated with mortality and HF rehospitalisation (HFH). 2 , 7 , 11 – 13 In older AHF patients with HFpEF and multiple comorbidities (chronic kidney disease, cancer, inflammatory conditions), the ALBIMED-HF study reported an even higher prevalence of hypoalbuminaemia (76.5%). 13 

Anatomy of the Liver and Liver Circulation 

The liver is a unique organ, with a complex, dual afferent blood supply (portal vein and hepatic artery [HA]) that receives approximately 25% of the cardiac output (CO). 14 The portal vein supplies 75–80% of blood (deoxygenated, but nutrient-rich) to the liver. 15 In contrast, the HA supplies approximately 25% of (oxygen-rich) blood to the liver, delivering up to 50% of liver’s oxygen requirements. The HA is the sole source of oxygenated blood to the bile ducts and peribiliary plexus. 14 

Blood from the hepatic sinusoids drains into the central veins and subsequently into the hepatic veins, which empty directly into the inferior vena cava. Because hepatic veins lack valves, elevations in inferior caval or right atrial pressure (RAP) are transmitted retrogradely to the hepatic sinusoids without attenuation. 16 These zonal differences determine hepatocyte function and underlie the differential susceptibility to hypoperfusion and ischaemic injury in AHF ( Figure 1 ). 

Total hepatic blood flow (HBF) is maintained constant through two interrelated physiological processes: the HA buffer response, in which a change in venous pressure leads to an inverse change in arterial flow; and HA autoregulation, in which HA flow is maintained constant despite changes in systemic pressure. 17 The HA buffer response allows the HA to increase its flow when portal flow decreases; however, this compensatory mechanism is limited in systemic hypotension and right-sided congestion. 15 

The liver’s dual supply and zonal architecture explain why venous congestion leads to centrilobular fibrosis, whereas severe hypoperfusion results in ischaemic hepatitis. 18 , 19 

Pathophysiology of Liver Dysfunction in Acute Heart Failure 

Interpreting LFT abnormalities in AHF requires an understanding of the hepatic origin of LFTs ( Figure 1 ). Each biomarker provides complementary insights into hepatocellular integrity, cholestasis and synthetic capacity, with distinct origins and clinical relevance. Biomarkers should be interpreted in a clinical context because their specificity is limited and many confounding factors influence their trajectory (e.g. synthetic function parameters are modified by anticoagulants, antibiotics, malnutrition and vitamin K deficiency). 

Mechanisms of Liver Dysfunction in Acute Heart Failure 

Venous Congestion 

Right-sided HF, severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and elevated central venous pressure (CVP) increase intrasinusoidal pressure, leading to reduced perivenular blood flow, perisinusoidal oedema that may impair oxygenation and, ultimately, hepatocyte atrophy and cholestasis. 17 , 20 , 21 Chronic congestion produces perisinusoidal fibrosis and, in advanced stages, congestive hepatopathy ( Figure 3 ). In AHF, rapid increases in venous pressure trigger abrupt elevations in BIL and GGT. Factors contributing to elevated BIL include bile canalicular compression or obstruction due to distended hepatic veins and congested sinusoids, hepatocellular dysfunction, haemolysis and the use of concomitant medications. 22 It should be noted that congestion alone cannot cause significant liver necrosis. Sherlock found no correlation between RAP and the degree of perivenular necrosis in patients with severe congestive HF. 20 However, chronic congestive HF predisposes the liver to acute ischaemic injury. 23 

Notably, sustained hepatic congestion may also stimulate the production of inflammatory biomarkers (e.g. GDF-15), which have been implicated in the development of anorexia and cachexia in advanced stages of HF. 24 

Systemic venous congestion in AHF involves both the liver and the splanchnic circulation, leading to intestinal oedema and increased gut wall permeability. 25 , 26 The gut–liver axis describes the bidirectional relationship between the gut microbiota, intestinal barrier and liver function. 27 Changes in microbiota composition allow bacterial translocation and endotoxin release, contributing to systemic inflammation and exacerbating hepatic injury. 27 , 28 The gut–liver–heart interaction may amplify congestion-related organ dysfunction and represents an additional mechanism for right-sided haemodynamic alterations and worsening liver function during AHF. Hypoperfusion 

Low CO and hypotension reduce total HBF ( Figure 3 ). Centrilobular hepatocytes (zone 3) are most susceptible to ischaemic injury, resulting in ischaemic hepatitis (IH; shock liver or hypoxic hepatitis), characterised by massive elevations in transaminase (up to >20 times the ULN). 18 To note, hepatic necrosis is rarely the result of hypoperfusion alone, and in addition to reduced HBF, many other contributors, such as congestion, hypoxemia and inflammation, increase susceptibility to hepatic necrosis. 18 

Toxic Agents 

In AHF, neurohormonal activation, mitochondrial dysfunction and systemic inflammation expose the liver to circulating cytokines and oxidative stress, which amplify hepatocellular injury. 29 Drugs frequently used in AHF may also contribute to cytopathic hypoxia, either by direct hepatotoxicity (amiodarone, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, statins) or via an indirect mechanism (milrinone [vasodilation with subsequent hepatic hypoperfusion], vasopressors [splanchnic vasoconstriction with hepatic hypoperfusion] and diuretics [volume depletion]). 30 – 34 

In addition, hypoalbuminaemia, caused by multifactorial mechanisms in AHF, may alter drug pharmacokinetics by modifying protein binding, potentially affecting the efficacy and safety of commonly used therapies, including diuretics; this is considered to contribute to loop diuretic resistance because ALB–loop diuretic binding facilitates drug delivery to the kidney. 35 , 36

Hepatocellular Nutritional Status 

In the setting of low HBF with overlapping venous congestion, the resulting widened oxygen gradient increases hepatocyte oxygen extraction in zone 1, thereby further lowering oxygen tension in zone 3, the most hypoxic region. 37 Hence, the metabolic and nutritional status of hepatocytes in this area is impaired, rendering them critically vulnerable to injury, which manifests as disproportionate elevations in aminotransferases and reduced ALB synthesis during low-flow states. Lower ALT levels may be seen in patients with advanced disease because a poor cellular metabolic status secondary to cachexia, including shifts in amino acid metabolism, can affect enzyme production rates, including ALT. 9 , 17 , 20 

Oxygen Mismatch in Septic Shock 

In patients with IH due to septic shock, CO and oxygen delivery are preserved, and sometimes even increased, due to low systemic vascular resistance. 19 Indeed, the hepatic injury in this scenario is a consequence of increased oxygen demand and the inability to extract and use it. Therefore, a systemic inflammatory storm, even without changes in CO, arterial oxygen saturation, or RAP, can cause severe liver damage. Extrapolating, the inflammatory burden in HF is overwhelming and, when combined with baseline circulatory perturbations, can further exacerbate hepatic disturbances. 

Similarly, other conditions that cause severe systemic hypoxemia, such as severe pulmonary oedema (PE), pneumonia and acute respiratory distress, may present a biochemical pattern resembling IH (i.e. massive elevations in AST, ALT and lactate dehydrogenase), even in the presence of normal CO and only mild elevations in right-sided pressures. 19 

Previous Hepatic Disease 

An intrinsic hepatopathy (e.g. viral hepatitis, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease) that compromises the defensive mechanisms of the liver significantly diminishes the threshold needed for haemodynamic disturbances to produce severe liver dysfunction. The main processes involved in HBF typically create a robust anti-ischaemic defence mechanism, but changes in their function alter the entire perspective on cardiohepatic interactions. This contributes to more severe and prolonged abnormal LFTs, with poorer recovery trajectories and higher mortality risk. 

Duration of the Underlying Cardiovascular Disease or Heart Failure 

In patients with chronic HF, the structural pathological changes in the liver are correlated with the duration of the cardiovascular disease and the circulatory disturbances. 20 Given structural and functional liver changes induced by inflammation, the liver becomes vulnerable to even less severe AHF precipitants. Significant LFT abnormalities in incipient HF without significant haemodynamic disturbances primarily reflect the severity of the acute event. 

In contrast, in patients with chronic, long-lasting HF, the magnitude of LFT abnormalities reflects the severity of the underlying disease. A particular clinical setting is low ALT levels in patients with sarcopenia and cachexia. ALT is also present in skeletal muscles, and a decrease in muscle mass due to cachexia may therefore reduce the ALT released into the bloodstream. 9 , 17 , 20 

Hepatic Biological Profiles 

The biochemical profiles of various types of hepatic dysfunction in AHF are important to establish because LFTs are more than passive markers: they are the first indicators of disease stage and reflect complex haemodynamic and systemic derangements (congestion, hypoperfusion and right ventricular [RV] dysfunction). Abnormal LFTs in AHF closely reflect underlying haemodynamic derangements ( Table 1 ). The relative predominance of congestion versus hypoperfusion determines the biochemical profile: predominantly cholestatic in systemic venous hypertension (elevated BIL, ALP, GGT), compared with hepatocellular injury in reduced forward flow (a sharp rise in aminotransferases). 

Cholestatic Profile 

Cholestasis (elevated BIL and ALP or GGT, minimal elevation of ALT and AST) is the typical pattern of LFT abnormalities in AHF, primarily driven by passive hepatic congestion resulting from increased CVP ( Table 1 ). One key contributor to this backward pressure is severe TR, which exacerbates RAP. Excessive preload, characteristic of TR, may concomitantly increase pulmonary venous pressure and RV afterload through a pathophysiological cascade of hepatic and renal congestion, leading to increased sodium, fluid retention and cholestatic injury. 

In AHF, elevations in BIL were haemodynamically correlated with increased CVP and decreased CO. 38 In the EVEREST and the PROTECT trials, elevated BIL and GGT were interpreted as reflecting persistent congestion with a higher CVP, serving as surrogates for the severity of right-sided HF. 2 , 7 The improvements in LFTs observed during hospitalisation, due to proper decongestion and reduction of severe TR, were associated with better outcomes, positioning LFTs not only as diagnostic tools, but also as dynamic markers of the therapeutic response and clinical trajectory. 9 , 39 

Cytolytic Profile 

Either hepatic congestion or direct liver injury through the activation of inflammatory cascades, oxidative stress or other mechanisms can result in hepatic damage. Serelaxin administration in the RELAX-AHF trial was associated with reduced transaminase levels, and these changes have been linked to improved survival in AHF. 40 In the ASCEND-HF trial, the elevations in AST and ALT were small, and no relationship was observed between aminotransferase levels and baseline characteristics. 

Aminotransferases were not associated with outcomes, because hypotension alone is not always sufficient to elevate aminotransferase levels. 4 As noted above, for severe hepatocellular damage to occur, the haemodynamic disturbances must be important, reflecting either a more advanced stage of the cardiac disease or a more severe acute cardiovascular insult. 

Impaired Synthesis 

Hypoalbuminaemia in HF has been linked to hepatic dysfunction, inflammatory stress, poor tolerance to diuretic and neurohormonal therapies, malnutrition and haemodilution. 2 , 7 From a pathophysiological perspective, reduced ALB levels lower plasma oncotic pressure, thereby further augmenting hydrostatic-mediated extravasation into the interstitium and subsequently aggravating systemic and hepatic congestion. 35 

Another prevalent biochemical abnormality encountered in AHF is an increased INR, reflecting hepatic insufficiency (caused by systemic inflammation, neurohormonal activation and venous congestion). RV dysfunction was independently associated with markers reflecting synthetic liver function, such as prolonged INR and reduced ALB levels. 41 Despite its prevalence, INR may be a stronger predictor of adverse outcomes when a threshold is imposed, but further research is needed. 42 

Markers of Reversibility Versus Irreversibility of Liver Function Test Abnormalities 

Specific AHF therapies (decongestion and restoration of CO) can reverse abnormal LFTs (days to weeks) or, conversely, induce irreversible organ damage (depending on the mechanism, the magnitude of dysfunction, the nature and duration of haemodynamic compromise, pre-existing cardiac or liver disease and recovery of perfusion or congestion). 43 Currently, there are no absolute laboratory thresholds of LFTs that can reliably differentiate reversible from irreversible hepatic injury in AHF. 

Persistent LFT abnormalities during hospitalisation despite optimising therapy may indicate chronic liver disease, advanced cardiohepatic syndrome or even the need for prompt mechanical circulatory support (MCS). 4 Thus, monitoring LFT trajectories rather than focusing solely on abnormal values is critically important for better understanding these biomarkers and informing prognostic interventions. 

In congestive hepatopathy, moderate BIL elevation (less than 3–5 mg/dl) and cholestatic enzymes may improve with decongestion. Persistent hyperbilirubinaemia despite therapy suggests more chronic or irreversible damage. In IH, very high aminotransferases (more than 20–50 times the ULN, often >2,000 U/l) can still normalise rapidly (7–10 days) if perfusion is restored. Synthetic markers (ALB <3.0 g/dl, prolonged INR >1.5–2.0) are more strongly associated with irreversible or advanced disease because they reflect structural or functional loss. However, the thresholds to discriminate between reversible and irreversible injury are not absolute. Reversibility is best judged in the clinical context, considering historical LFT values, the trajectory of LFTs after haemodynamic optimisation and their correlation with markers of liver synthetic function. To note, multiorgan injury characteristic of AHF may complicate the clinical picture and, along with abnormal LFTs, other organs may release injury enzymes, such as pancreatic enzymes. 44 

The distinction between cardiohepatic dysfunction and primary liver disease is challenging because no single test or isolated biochemical pattern can help differentiate between the two conditions, particularly in the elderly. In addition, differential diagnosis is difficult in HFpEF and chronic right-sided HF, where cholestatic patterns are common and may coexist with or mimic primary hepatic disease, requiring a multidisciplinary approach. In clinical practice, the diagnosis should rely on a combined approach that integrates clinical context, haemodynamic profile and the temporal trajectory of LFT abnormalities, rather than on isolated laboratory findings. 

Evidence of systemic venous congestion, right-sided HF, elevated filling pressures, or reduced CO strongly favours a cardiac origin of hepatic injury. The improvement in LFTs following effective decongestion or restoration of perfusion supports a cardiohepatic mechanism, whereas persistent or progressive abnormalities despite haemodynamic optimisation should raise the suspicion of intrinsic liver disease. 

Prognosis 

LFT abnormalities carry prognostic significance in AHF, being independently associated with HFH, cardiovascular death and all-cause mortality ( Table 2 ). 

Evidence From Registries 

In the RO-AHFS registry, the highest quartile of admission ALT values (65–205 IU/l) was independently associated with higher in-hospital all-cause mortality. 39 In the ESC-HF-LT registry, liver dysfunction (6% of patients) was more prevalent among HFrEF patients. 45 Among non-cardiac comorbidities, liver dysfunction carried the highest 1-year all-cause mortality risk, emphasising its prognostic weight and need for early identification. 45 

The persistence of abnormal LFTs or the worsening of LFTs during hospitalisation identifies a population at higher risk of rehospitalisation and death. 2 , 3 , 7 

Evidence From Randomised Clinical Trials 

In the ASCEND-HF trial, elevated BIL (≥1.2 mg/dl) and low ALB (<3.5 g/dl) have been strong predictors of short-term adverse outcomes (30- and 180-day mortality). 4 A subanalysis of 3,813 patients enrolled in the EVEREST trial found that elevated baseline BIL and low ALB were independently associated with increased 30- and 90-day mortality and worse long-term outcomes, and that their improvement during hospitalisation was associated with better 60-day and 1-year outcomes. 7 

In the RELAX-AHF trial, increases in AST and ALT were associated with high 180-day all-cause mortality. 40 In a post hoc analysis of the SURVIVE trial, the most prevalent LFT abnormality was a transaminase elevation in 26% of patients, despite patients with blood pressure <85 mmHg (most susceptible to cytolytic disturbances) being excluded from the study. 3 Aminotransferase elevation was a strong independent predictor of short- and long-term mortality, and was correlated with the severity of HF. 3 

A secondary analysis of the PROTECT trial showed that GGT and BIL were correlated with persistent congestion, in-hospital events and early HFH. 2 GGT shows promise as an independent prognostic marker in AHF, although the evidence varies across studies. The STRONG-HF trial analysed the impact of LFT abnormalities during rapid intensive uptitration of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), showing that baseline elevated BIL and transaminases were associated with greater congestion and lower tolerance to GDMT (more adverse effects, inadequate response), thereby affecting the efficacy and safety of optimal HF therapy. 9 Moreover, persistent elevations in BIL 90 days after discharge were associated with a higher risk of 180-day all-cause death or HFH. 9 Except for ALT, changes in LFTs after discharge were associated with relief of congestion and a good prognosis. 9 One remarkable finding from STRONG-HF is that, unlike BIL, lower ALT levels at hospital discharge were associated with greater congestion and a trend towards worse outcomes. 9 This finding is similar to that of ASCEND-HF, in which higher ALT was associated with less peripheral oedema, challenging the belief that patients with high ALT are more congested and decompensated. 4 Considering the complex interplay between reduced ALT expression due to sarcopenia and increased ALT expression from liver failure, it is crucial to interpret ALT changes in the clinical context. 9 

Recently, in a study of 7,284 acute cardiac patients (of whom 1,165 had CS), the elevation of both BIL and ALP was independently associated with in-hospital mortality (17.9% versus 6.4%; adjusted OR 1.54; 95% CI [1.27–1.86]; p<0.001) and long-term mortality. 47 

Hypoalbuminaemia (ALB <3.4 g/dl) has been extensively studied as a prognostic marker in AHF. Hypoalbuminaemia confers a more than twofold increase in mortality risk, being independently associated with higher 1-year mortality and with increased 180-day mortality when low ALB coexists with elevated AST and ALT. 48 Conflicting data have been reported, with no association between ALB levels and in-hospital endpoints, long-term endpoints, symptomatic changes, or changes in cardiorenal biomarkers reported in the DOSE-AHF and ROSE-AHF trials. 35 In the COAPT trial, hypoalbuminaemia was found to increase the risk of all-cause mortality, but not HFH, all-cause hospitalisation, and the composite of all-cause mortality and HFH. 49 

Recently, a meta-analysis of 30,623 patients with HFpEF (16 studies) found that higher ALB levels are associated with better outcomes. 50 

Impaired synthetic capacity (low ALB, prolonged INR) is also a powerful prognostic marker. Elevated INR was an independent marker of all-cause mortality in non-anticoagulated patients admitted with AHF, reflecting both hepatic dysfunction and haemostatic abnormalities. 42 

Ratios, Scores and Their Clinical Utility 

Several ratios and composite scores have been evaluated to improve the interpretation of LFT abnormalities, potentially enhancing diagnostic, differential diagnostic and prognostic accuracy ( Table 3 ). In IH, lactate dehydrogenase increases to extremely high levels, exceeding ALT levels, with an ALT/lactate dehydrogenase ratio <1.5. 43 , 51 The ALB–BIL score encompasses both liver fibrosis and fluid overload; it may be suitable for assessing liver dysfunction in AHF and for providing prognostic information. 52 The Model for End-stage Liver Disease (MELD) score and its modification, MELD-XI, are prognostic tools initially used for liver disease and now recognised for predicting outcomes in AHF. 5 , 53 , 54 These scores assess the risk of multiple organ dysfunction linking liver and kidney dysfunction. These scores also identify risk in AHF patients supported by left ventricular assist devices (LVADs) or undergoing cardiac surgery. 7 , 55 – 60 The AST/ALT ratio is a routinely available liver fibrosis severity score in patients with liver disease. In ischaemic or toxic liver injury, AST levels usually peak before those of ALT because of the enzyme’s peculiar intralobular distribution. 43 , 61 

The Fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) index was developed for chronic liver fibrosis, but recent data suggest that it may have a role in AHF. In a study of 1,468 AHF patients, a higher admission FIB-4 was independently associated with 365-day all-cause mortality and HF events. 62 However, FIB-4 is not yet a standard clinical marker in AHF, because the component values (AST, ALT, platelets) can reflect acute injury, drug effects and haemolysis, not just congestion, and potential confounders may affect FIB-4 values. 

Clinical Settings 

Abnormal LFTs are observed in any clinical profile of AHF, including hypertensive AHF. 63 Residual congestion and organ vascular dysregulation in HFrEF patients with a history of HFH make them vulnerable to usual triggers, such as high systolic blood pressure, resulting in multiorgan dysfunction. 64 , 65 This reinforces that hepatic injury in AHF is not exclusive to hypotensive states, and may result from vascular mismatch and organ congestion. 

In acute coronary syndrome and AHF, the diagnosis and prognostic value of LFT abnormalities remains challenging due to overlapping pathophysiology. ALT originates primarily from hepatocytes and reflects hepatic injury, whereas AST levels may also increase after ischaemic injury in cardiac, renal or skeletal muscle, limiting specificity. 66 Interpreting transaminase elevations and the De Ritis ratio (AST/ALT) requires additional biomarkers (troponin or creatine kinase) for risk stratification. 66 – 69 However, elevations in AST after acute coronary syndrome are usually transient, with levels returning to normal within 48–72 hours, unless there is ongoing haemodynamic compromise. 66 

In patients with AHF and PE, LFT abnormalities reflect the extent of PE, the severity of hypoxemia and RV dysfunction. Although hypoxic hepatitis is rare (1%), very high AST and ALT levels were seen in massive PE. ALB was lower in patients with RV dysfunction. 70 

When AHF is secondary to valvular heart disease, abnormal LFTs are the result of RV dysfunction, the severity of TR and pulmonary hypertension. 71 , 72 Persistently high direct BIL levels are commonly associated with fixed pulmonary hypertension. 

In patients with acute aortic syndromes and AHF, abnormal LFTs, particularly ALT and AST, suggest the development of tamponade. 73 

Therapeutic Options 

The management of AHF, with or without liver dysfunction at admission, follows the general recommendations based on the AHF phenotype, with specific recommendations regarding precipitants (acute coronary syndrome or ischaemia, arrhythmia, hypertensive crisis, infection or sepsis, PE, mechanical complications, cardiac tamponade). 74 Marked transaminase elevations may suggest shock liver, requiring prompt initiation or escalation of haemodynamic support (inotropes, vasopressors and temporary MCS). Elevated markers of cholestasis may require a more aggressive decongestion or investigation for RV dysfunction or TR, because cholestatic enzymes, but not transaminases, were found to be related to RV dysfunction, after adjustment for several clinical variables. 42 

A mixed pattern is often encountered and therapeutic management should be individualised accordingly. 

Decongestion 

Relief of congestion remains the cornerstone of AHF management. Elevated BIL was associated with poor decongestion, and hyperbilirubinaemia at discharge was predictive of early rehospitalisation, suggesting that improper decongestion portends a worse prognosis. 2 Similarly, post-discharge persistent BIL elevations were associated with a higher risk of 180-day all-cause death or HFH. 9 Proper decongestion in AHF induces dynamic changes in LFTs, resulting in progressive reductions in BIL, ALP and GGT, reflecting improved hepatobiliary clearance. ALT and AST, often elevated due to IH or severe congestion, will normalise more slowly, depending on the rate of hepatocellular recovery. Improvements in synthetic function are less immediate ( Table 4 ). When measured serially during hospitalisation, LFTs can be considered sensitive markers for monitoring in-hospital clinical course and therapy response. 

Low ALB levels may impair the delivery of loop diuretics to the proximal tubule, contributing to diuretic resistance, and exogenous ALB administration can restore drug delivery, increase oncotic pressure and overcome diuretic resistance. 36 , 75 However, routine ALB supplementation remains controversial because multiple mechanisms of hypoalbuminaemia are involved in AHF, including haemodilution, malnutrition and hepatic dysfunction, which may be reversed after effective decongestion and nutrition correction. 35 , 75 In advanced HF and in inflammatory states, vascular permeability is high. Infused ALB often leaks into the interstitial space (third spacing), which can actually worsen peripheral oedema rather than contributing to volume expansion. Rapid ALB administration can lead to volume overload and pulmonary oedema, particularly in patients with intravascular congestion. 76 , 77 It may be considered selectively in those with refractory oedema or ascites, despite maximum diuretic therapy and in whom hypoalbuminaemia is profound. 77 Individualised management that addresses congestion, inflammation and triggers, rather than targeting serum ALB levels alone, is reasonable. 

Improving Perfusion 

In a hypoperfusion state, restoring CO with inotropes, vasopressors or MCS is required because IH may be rapidly reversed and corrected. Inotropes and vasopressors exert distinct effects on LFTs. By improving CO, agents such as dobutamine or milrinone may enhance hepatic oxygen delivery, leading to a decline in transaminases when hypoxic injury predominates. Conversely, vasopressors, such as noradrenaline, can worsen hepatic perfusion by splanchnic vasoconstriction, potentially aggravating BIL elevations or prolonging the prothrombin time. Re-elevations of AST and ALT may reflect transient ischaemia–reperfusion. Overall, the evolution of LFTs during inotrope or vasopressor therapy reveals the balance between improved systemic perfusion and drug-induced hepatic vasoconstriction, underscoring the importance of monitoring treatment safety ( Table 5 ). 

Evidence from registries (RO-AHFS Registry) and trials (PROTECT, EVEREST, STRONG-HF) shows that persistent deterioration of LFTs during hospitalisation is associated with failure to wean inotropes and a poor short-term prognosis. 2 , 7 , 9 , 39 Inotrope dependence represents the clinical situation in which AHF patients cannot sustain haemodynamic stability or organ perfusion after withdrawal of inotrope therapy, reflecting advanced myocardial dysfunction and impaired systemic reserve. In CS, increases in serum transaminase levels were associated with higher 180-day all-cause mortality. 40 Abnormal LFTs often signal inotrope dependence by indicating inadequate CO and persistent hepatic congestion. Elevated BIL and aminotransferases indicate reduced hepatic clearance and hypoxic injury, whereas ALP and GGT mirror cholestasis from elevated CVP. Notably, increasing total BIL during attempted weaning was correlated with increased CVP and decreased CO, and was the strongest predictor of adverse outcomes for both the composite endpoint of cardiovascular death or HFH (HR 1.21 per 1-SD increase; p<0.0001) and all-cause mortality (HR 1.19 per 1-SD increase; p<0.0001). 38 Thus, elevated BIL, as a surrogate of systemic congestion and limited forward flow, may identify patients at high risk of recurrent decompensation or death. In CS, shock liver frequently develops as part of multiple organ dysfunction. Short-term MCS, including intra-aortic balloon pump, Impella and veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, can restore systemic perfusion while variably influencing congestion, thus ameliorating liver dysfunction. The intra-aortic balloon pump and Impella primarily improve forward flow, leading to rapid declines in aminotransferases when IH predominates. Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, although effective in stabilising CO, may increase right-sided pressures, thereby delaying the normalisation of BIL and cholestatic markers. 78 Thus, LFT trajectories during MCS reflect the balance between enhanced perfusion and persistent congestion, serving as markers of end-organ injury, organ recovery and prognosis. 

Correction of Severe Tricuspid Regurgitation 

Tricuspid transcatheter valve replacement effectively reduces severe TR and improves ALT, AST and BIL, with greater benefits in patients with baseline liver dysfunction. 79 , 80 These findings underscore the reversibility of TR-related liver dysfunction and the potential role of tricuspid transcatheter valve replacement in promoting hepatic recovery. 79 , 80 In the TRILUMINATE trial, improvements in LFT abnormalities (MELD-XI score) were observed only in patients with successful (less than moderate residual TR) tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair. 81 The persistence of LFT abnormalities was associated with increased mortality and more HFH at the 12-month follow-up after tricuspid transcatheter edge-to-edge repair. 82 

Advanced and Experimental Therapies for Acute Heart Failure 

The use of LVADs in advanced HF patients alters hepatic haemodynamics. Preimplantation elevations in BIL and ALP or prolonged INR are associated with a more advanced disease, long-standing hepatic congestion, subsequent post-LVAD RV failure and increased early mortality. 59 LVAD implantation improves forward flow and systemic perfusion, leading to partial normalisation of LFTs caused by hypoperfusion. The persistence or worsening of cholestatic or synthetic dysfunction markers after LVAD (an elevation of at least two times the ULN in AST and ALT or total BIL >2.0 mg/dl) may suggest RV dysfunction. 83 This scenario is strongly and specifically correlated with early RV failure, as well as with prolonged intensive care needs and increased mortality. 84 One peculiar issue seen with LVADs and LFTs is that a cholestatic picture persists initially, even as haemodynamic restoration occurs. Experimental Acute Heart Failure Therapies 

The role of liver ‘depuration’ or extracorporeal support strategies (ALB dialysis, plasma exchange or haemadsorption) is limited and routine extracorporeal liver support in AHF is not recommended. 74 Emerging data suggest that targeting inflammation may improve hepatic injury in AHF; trials of anti-inflammatory agents and corticosteroids are underway. 86 , 87 Hepatoprotective strategies remain experimental. 

Liver Dysfunction and Implementation of Guideline-directed Medical Therapy 

Data from the STRONG-HF trial suggest that patients with severe liver disease could undergo successful uptitration of GDMT and that the benefits of high-intensity care persisted regardless of baseline hepatic dysfunction. 9 These observations support a proactive approach to therapy rather than withholding treatment based on LFTs alone. 88 In addition, liver dysfunction was not perceived by cardiologists as a barrier to the implementation of GDMT. 89 

Evolution of Liver Function Tests During Hospitalisation and the Early Post-discharge Period 

The clinical course of LFTs is closely tied to the effectiveness of decongestive therapy ( Table 4 ). Successful decongestion with IV diuretics or vasodilators typically results in a rapid improvement in BIL and ALP levels within days, with normalisation occurring over several weeks. Patients who achieved greater reductions in congestion also experienced parallel improvements in hepatic markers, correlated with outcomes. 40 Intensive uptitration of GDMT, leading to robust decongestion, significantly reduced LFT abnormalities. 9 Incomplete relief of congestion predicts residual hepatic dysfunction and adverse prognosis. 

By restoring perfusion, inotropes can accelerate the resolution of IH and aminotransferase elevations. However, their use usually signals severe circulatory compromise. Data from the ASCEND-HF trial showed that patients receiving inotropes had higher baseline LFT derangements and worse long-term outcomes, reflecting the underlying disease severity rather than a drug effect. 4 

The persistence of LFT abnormalities during hospitalisation or after discharge is associated with a poor prognosis. Aminotransferase elevations from acute hypoperfusion often normalise within 7–14 days once perfusion improves. In contrast, BIL and cholestatic abnormalities may persist for weeks, particularly in patients with ongoing right-sided congestion or structural liver changes. Miyama et al. reported that nearly one-third of patients discharged with abnormal LFTs continued to exhibit abnormalities at long-term follow-up (12 months), strongly associated with mortality. 90 Longitudinal data suggest that up to 25–30% of patients exhibit persistent abnormalities beyond 3 months, particularly those with chronic right-sided failure, advanced fibrosis or repeated episodes of HF decompensation. 90 

Worsening LFT abnormalities during AHF hospitalisation are commonly caused by insufficient decongestion, where a suboptimal diuretic response or premature discharge leads to residual high right-sided pressures that perpetuate hepatic dysfunction. Similarly, a premature transition from IV to oral therapies may lead to early fluid regain, driving a rebound in congestion-related hepatopathy. Another contributor is inotrope tolerance. Patients requiring prolonged inotrope support may initially exhibit improved perfusion; however, over time, tachyphylaxis, arrhythmias and increased oxygen demand can counteract the benefits, ultimately worsening hepatic ischaemia. In addition, toxic agents, including vasodilators or hepatotoxic drugs used in comorbid conditions, can further amplify hepatic injury. In rare instances, amiodarone may induce acute liver injury or worsen liver dysfunction in CS patients with multiple organ dysfunction. 30 Overall, worsening LFTs in AHF signal an unstable trajectory, often identifying patients at higher short-term risk of rehospitalisation and mortality. 2 , 3 , 7 Recognising this pattern should prompt re-evaluation of decongestion strategies, medication transitions and potential drug toxicity, ensuring tailored therapy to restore hepatic function and improve outcomes. 

Gaps in Knowledge 

Despite increasing recognition of the prognostic and pathophysiological relevance of LFT abnormalities in AHF, several major knowledge gaps persist, limiting translation into routine clinical practice and therapeutic decision-making ( Table 6 ). These knowledge gaps include the lack of standardised thresholds for and classification of liver abnormalities in AHF; limited mechanistic studies linking congestion, hypoperfusion, inflammation and hepatic fibrosis progression; few interventional studies directly targeting hepatic injury; a poor understanding of the long-term hepatic consequences of recurrent AHF episodes; and the under-representation of HFpEF populations in biomarker studies. 50 

Conclusion 

LFT abnormalities are common in AHF and reflect haemodynamic status, therapeutic response and prognosis. They arise from overlapping mechanisms of congestion, hypoperfusion and systemic inflammation, with distinct biochemical signatures. Elevated BIL and transaminases, hypoalbuminaemia and prolonged INR predict adverse outcomes. Recognition of these abnormalities should prompt clinicians to optimise decongestion, improve perfusion and oxygenation and consider the potential for systemic inflammation. 

Future work should refine the definition of cardiohepatic syndrome, validate therapeutic strategies and integrate liver biomarkers into routine risk stratification to improve outcomes for patients with AHF.
